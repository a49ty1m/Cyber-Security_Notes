# WiFi Fundamentals

**Roadmap:** Part 1 — Fundamentals → Stage 5 — Wireless & Physical Connections → WiFi Fundamentals → 802.11 Standards, Frequency Bands & Channel Allocation

# Section 1 — What it is and where it sits

WiFi is a wireless LAN technology based primarily on the **IEEE 802.11 family of standards**. Instead of carrying Ethernet frames through copper, an access point and wireless clients exchange frames over radio frequencies. The important fundamentals for security work are the relationship between **802.11 generations, frequency bands, channels, channel width, modulation, range, and compatibility**.

For a penetration tester, this knowledge produces a mental model of what wireless networks actually look like at the RF and protocol level. It lets you recognize whether a network is operating on 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, or 6 GHz, understand why certain clients can or cannot connect, and identify the channel and capabilities of an access point before moving into wireless assessment.

```text
Physical / Network Fundamentals
            ↓
      Ethernet / TCP-IP
            ↓
   Stage 5 — Wireless
            ↓
      WiFi Fundamentals
            ↓
802.11 → Bands → Channels → Channel Width
            ↓
  AP / Client Discovery
            ↓
Wireless Security Assessment
            ↓
Authentication / Encryption / Attacks
```

If you skip this, wireless testing becomes tool-driven guessing: you may scan the wrong interface, misunderstand channel behavior, misinterpret an AP's capabilities, or waste time assuming that every WiFi network behaves like a traditional 2.4 GHz network.

The previous networking fundamentals explain how hosts communicate; this topic adds the **radio and 802.11 layer** that allows those hosts to communicate without a cable.

# Section 2 — How attackers actually use this

## 2.1 First determine what wireless technology is actually present

An attacker does not begin by assuming:

> "This is WiFi, therefore it is 2.4 GHz."

They first establish the wireless environment.

They are interested in:

* SSIDs
* BSSIDs
* frequency band
* operating channel
* channel width
* signal strength
* supported 802.11 generations
* security configuration
* AP vendor information
* client devices
* hidden or less obvious networks
* neighboring networks
* channel congestion
* whether an AP supports 2.4, 5, or 6 GHz
* whether the same network is advertised across multiple bands

A simplified observation might look like:

```text
SSID:        Corp-WiFi
BSSID:       AA:BB:CC:11:22:33
Band:        5 GHz
Channel:     36
Width:       80 MHz
Standard:    802.11ac/ax
Signal:      -48 dBm
Security:    WPA2/WPA3
```

That information immediately changes the assessment strategy.

A 5 GHz 802.11ac/ax network is fundamentally different from an old 2.4 GHz 802.11b/g network in terms of channels, bandwidth, range, and supported capabilities.

---

## 2.2 Identify the 802.11 generation

The attacker wants to know which generation an AP supports because the generation gives clues about:

* maximum theoretical throughput
* frequency compatibility
* channel widths
* modulation capabilities
* supported features
* likely client compatibility
* whether legacy behavior is present

The major generations are:

| Standard | Common name | Primary band | Major characteristic           |
| -------- | ----------- | ------------ | ------------------------------ |
| 802.11a  | WiFi 2      | 5 GHz        | Older 5 GHz WiFi               |
| 802.11b  | WiFi 1      | 2.4 GHz      | Legacy low-speed WiFi          |
| 802.11g  | WiFi 3      | 2.4 GHz      | Faster 2.4 GHz                 |
| 802.11n  | WiFi 4      | 2.4/5 GHz    | MIMO, wider channels           |
| 802.11ac | WiFi 5      | 5 GHz        | High-throughput 5 GHz          |
| 802.11ax | WiFi 6      | 2.4/5/6 GHz* | OFDMA, efficiency improvements |

*6 GHz operation is associated with **WiFi 6E**, which extends WiFi 6 into the 6 GHz band.

An attacker seeing:

```text
802.11n
802.11ac
802.11ax
```

should not treat these as three completely independent technologies.

An AP can advertise support for multiple generations simultaneously.

---

## 2.3 Determine the frequency band

The next question is:

> "Where in the RF spectrum is this network operating?"

### 2.4 GHz

2.4 GHz provides comparatively good range and penetration through obstacles, but the band is crowded.

Typical sources of interference include:

* neighboring WiFi networks
* Bluetooth
* wireless peripherals
* IoT devices
* other 2.4 GHz radios

The commonly used non-overlapping 20 MHz channels in many regulatory domains are:

```text
Channel 1       Channel 6       Channel 11
   ↓               ↓               ↓
[----]          [----]          [----]
```

The exact channel availability depends on the regulatory domain.

### 5 GHz

5 GHz generally provides:

* more available channels
* higher throughput
* less congestion than 2.4 GHz in many environments
* shorter effective range
* greater sensitivity to obstacles

It also introduces additional concepts such as **DFS channels**, where certain channels have requirements related to radar detection.

### 6 GHz

6 GHz provides a much larger amount of spectrum for compatible WiFi devices.

It is particularly useful for:

* high-density deployments
* wide channels
* WiFi 6E/7-capable clients
* reducing congestion from older WiFi generations

But older clients generally cannot simply connect to a 6 GHz-only network.

---

## 2.4 Understand channel allocation

A channel is a defined portion of the radio spectrum used for communication.

The attacker cares about:

```text
Frequency
   ↓
Channel
   ↓
Channel width
   ↓
Actual RF occupancy
```

For example:

```text
5 GHz
   ↓
Channel 36
   ↓
80 MHz channel width
   ↓
Higher potential throughput
```

Channel width matters because wider channels consume more spectrum.

Common widths include:

```text
20 MHz
40 MHz
80 MHz
160 MHz
```

A wider channel can increase throughput, but it also occupies more spectrum and can increase the chance of interference.

---

## 2.5 Distinguish channel number from frequency

A common beginner mistake is treating:

```text
Channel 36
```

and:

```text
5 GHz
```

as interchangeable.

They are not.

The **band** identifies the broad frequency range.

The **channel** identifies a specific operating portion within that range.

Conceptually:

```text
5 GHz band
│
├── Channel 36
├── Channel 40
├── Channel 44
├── Channel 48
├── ...
└── Other permitted channels
```

Therefore an assessment report should record both.

---

## 2.6 Look for multiple radios advertising the same SSID

A single WiFi network can have multiple AP radios.

For example:

```text
SSID: Corp-WiFi

        ┌── 2.4 GHz radio
        │     └── BSSID A
        │
Corp-WiFi
        │
        └── 5 GHz radio
              └── BSSID B
```

The SSID is the human-readable network name.

The BSSID identifies the individual wireless interface/radio advertising it.

This distinction becomes important when mapping enterprise wireless infrastructure.

---

## 2.7 Compare a dead-end finding with a high-value finding

### Dead-end finding

```text
SSID: HomeNetwork
Band: 2.4 GHz
Channel: 6
Signal: -78 dBm
```

This tells you something about the environment, but the weak signal makes it less immediately useful for an assessment.

It may simply be a neighboring network barely reaching your location.

### High-value finding

```text
SSID: Corp-WiFi
BSSID: AA:BB:CC:11:22:33
Band: 5 GHz
Channel: 36
Width: 80 MHz
Security: WPA2-Enterprise
Signal: -42 dBm
```

This is much more valuable because:

* the signal is strong
* it appears relevant to the target environment
* the operating band is known
* the AP can be mapped
* its security architecture is visible
* later wireless assessment can focus on authentication and client behavior

The important distinction is **relevance + accessibility + technical information**, not merely "I found an SSID."

---

## 2.8 Pivot from RF information into security assessment

The output of WiFi fundamentals becomes input to later stages.

```text
SSID / BSSID
     ↓
Band
     ↓
Channel
     ↓
Channel width
     ↓
802.11 capabilities
     ↓
Security configuration
     ↓
Authentication architecture
     ↓
Client/AP behavior
     ↓
Wireless security assessment
```

For example:

```text
Corp-WiFi
   ↓
5 GHz
   ↓
802.11ax
   ↓
80 MHz
   ↓
WPA2-Enterprise
   ↓
802.1X authentication
   ↓
Investigate authentication architecture
```

The attacker therefore does not treat channel discovery as the final objective.

It is **environment mapping that determines what to investigate next**.

# Section 3 — Core concepts and terminology

| Term              | Meaning                                                                                                            |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **802.11**        | IEEE family of standards defining wireless LAN communication.                                                      |
| **WiFi**          | Common industry term for interoperable wireless networking based on 802.11.                                        |
| **AP**            | Access Point; provides wireless network connectivity to clients.                                                   |
| **STA**           | Wireless station/client device.                                                                                    |
| **SSID**          | Human-readable wireless network name.                                                                              |
| **BSSID**         | Identifier for a specific wireless Basic Service Set, commonly represented by a MAC address.                       |
| **BSS**           | Basic Service Set; an AP and associated wireless stations.                                                         |
| **ESS**           | Extended Service Set; multiple APs providing a common WLAN.                                                        |
| **Channel**       | Specific frequency allocation used for wireless communication.                                                     |
| **Channel width** | Amount of spectrum occupied by a channel, such as 20, 40, 80, or 160 MHz.                                          |
| **2.4 GHz**       | Lower-frequency WiFi band with good range but significant congestion.                                              |
| **5 GHz**         | WiFi band offering more channels and generally higher throughput.                                                  |
| **6 GHz**         | Newer WiFi spectrum used by WiFi 6E and later technologies.                                                        |
| **RF**            | Radio Frequency; electromagnetic frequencies used for wireless communication.                                      |
| **MIMO**          | Multiple-Input Multiple-Output; uses multiple antennas to improve throughput/reliability.                          |
| **OFDMA**         | Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access; allows WiFi 6 to efficiently divide a channel among multiple users. |
| **DFS**           | Dynamic Frequency Selection; mechanisms required for certain 5 GHz channels to coexist with radar systems.         |
| **dBm**           | Logarithmic measurement of received signal power.                                                                  |
| **RSSI**          | Received Signal Strength Indicator; implementation-dependent measure of received signal strength.                  |
| **SNR**           | Signal-to-Noise Ratio; relationship between desired signal and background noise.                                   |
| **20 MHz**        | Narrow channel width commonly used for compatibility and reduced interference.                                     |
| **40 MHz**        | Channel width combining two 20 MHz portions.                                                                       |
| **80 MHz**        | Wider channel used by modern 5 GHz WiFi for higher throughput.                                                     |
| **160 MHz**       | Very wide channel providing greater theoretical throughput when spectrum permits.                                  |
| **WiFi 4**        | Marketing designation for 802.11n.                                                                                 |
| **WiFi 5**        | Marketing designation for 802.11ac.                                                                                |
| **WiFi 6**        | Marketing designation for 802.11ax.                                                                                |
| **WiFi 6E**       | WiFi 6 extended into the 6 GHz band.                                                                               |

### 802.11 generation map

| Generation | IEEE standard      | Typical band(s) | Important feature                    |
| ---------- | ------------------ | --------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| WiFi 1     | 802.11b            | 2.4 GHz         | Legacy high-compatibility WiFi       |
| WiFi 2     | 802.11a            | 5 GHz           | Early 5 GHz WiFi                     |
| WiFi 3     | 802.11g            | 2.4 GHz         | Higher-speed 2.4 GHz                 |
| WiFi 4     | 802.11n            | 2.4/5 GHz       | MIMO and channel bonding             |
| WiFi 5     | 802.11ac           | 5 GHz           | Wider channels and higher throughput |
| WiFi 6     | 802.11ax           | 2.4/5 GHz       | OFDMA and efficiency improvements    |
| WiFi 6E    | 802.11ax extension | 6 GHz           | WiFi 6 operation in 6 GHz            |

### Band comparison

| Property             | 2.4 GHz            | 5 GHz                 | 6 GHz                          |
| -------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Range                | Generally higher   | Generally lower       | Generally lower                |
| Congestion           | High               | Usually lower         | Potentially lower              |
| Legacy compatibility | Excellent          | Good                  | Limited to newer hardware      |
| Available spectrum   | Limited            | More                  | Much more                      |
| Typical modern use   | IoT/legacy/general | High-performance WiFi | Newer high-performance clients |

# Section 4 — Tools and commands

| Tool        | Command                                                                 | What it finds/shows                                       | When to use it                          |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| `iw`        | `iw dev`                                                                | Wireless interfaces and their state                       | Identify the WiFi adapter               |
| `iw`        | `iw dev wlan0 info`                                                     | Interface type, channel and frequency                     | Inspect one interface                   |
| `iw`        | `iw reg get`                                                            | Regulatory domain                                         | Check permitted RF configuration        |
| `iw`        | `iw list`                                                               | Adapter capabilities, bands, channels and widths          | Determine hardware capability           |
| `nmcli`     | `nmcli device wifi list`                                                | Nearby WiFi networks                                      | Quick passive environment survey        |
| `nmcli`     | `nmcli -f IN-USE,SSID,BSSID,CHAN,FREQ,SIGNAL,SECURITY device wifi list` | Structured AP information                                 | Quickly compare nearby networks         |
| `rfkill`    | `rfkill list`                                                           | Whether wireless radios are blocked                       | Troubleshoot missing WiFi               |
| `airmon-ng` | `sudo airmon-ng`                                                        | Wireless adapters and monitor-mode capability             | Identify compatible assessment hardware |
| `iw`        | `sudo iw dev wlan0 scan`                                                | AP advertisements, frequencies, channels and capabilities | Detailed authorized wireless discovery  |

### `iw dev`

```text
$ iw dev

phy#0
    Interface wlan0
        ifindex 3
        wdev 0x1
        addr aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff
        type managed
```

Interpretation:

```text
wlan0
  ↓
Wireless interface
  ↓
managed mode
  ↓
Currently operating as a normal WiFi client
```

---

### `iw dev wlan0 info`

```text
$ iw dev wlan0 info

Interface wlan0
    ifindex 3
    wdev 0x1
    addr aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff
    type managed
    channel 36 (5180 MHz), width: 80 MHz
```

The important values are:

```text
Channel: 36
Frequency: 5180 MHz
Width: 80 MHz
```

Therefore the interface is operating on the 5 GHz band.

---

### `iw reg get`

```text
$ iw reg get

global
country IN: DFS-...
    (2402 - 2482 @ ...)
    (5150 - 5350 @ ...)
```

The regulatory domain affects which frequencies and transmit parameters are legally and technically available.

---

### `iw list`

```text
$ iw list
Wiphy phy0
    Band 1:
        Frequencies:
            * 2412 MHz [1]
            * 2437 MHz [6]
            * 2462 MHz [11]

    Band 2:
        Frequencies:
            * 5180 MHz [36]
            * 5200 MHz [40]
            ...
```

This tells you what the **adapter itself supports**.

Do not confuse this with what a nearby AP supports.

---

### `nmcli device wifi list`

```text
$ nmcli device wifi list

SSID        BSSID              CHAN  FREQ      SIGNAL  SECURITY
Corp-WiFi   AA:BB:CC:11:22:33   36   5180 MHz    82    WPA2
Guest       DD:EE:FF:44:55:66    6   2437 MHz    61    WPA2
IoT         11:22:33:44:55:66  149   5745 MHz   45    WPA2
```

Interpretation:

```text
Corp-WiFi → 5 GHz → channel 36
Guest     → 2.4 GHz → channel 6
IoT       → 5 GHz → channel 149
```

This is a fast way to build a basic RF map.

---

### `rfkill list`

```text
$ rfkill list

0: phy0: Wireless LAN
    Soft blocked: no
    Hard blocked: no
```

Both values being `no` means the wireless radio is not blocked.

If you see:

```text
Soft blocked: yes
```

the operating system has disabled the radio.

---

### `airmon-ng`

```text
$ sudo airmon-ng

PHY     Interface    Driver       Chipset
phy0    wlan0        mt7921e      MediaTek ...
```

This identifies the wireless hardware and driver.

The important question is whether the hardware provides the capabilities required for the specific authorized assessment.

---

### `iw dev wlan0 scan`

```text
$ sudo iw dev wlan0 scan

BSS aa:bb:cc:11:22:33
    SSID: Corp-WiFi
    freq: 5180
    signal: -48.00 dBm
    capability: ...
    HT capabilities:
    VHT capabilities:
```

Interpretation:

```text
BSSID  → AA:BB:CC:11:22:33
SSID   → Corp-WiFi
Freq   → 5180 MHz
Signal → -48 dBm
```

The presence of HT/VHT/HE capability information can indicate support for progressively newer 802.11 feature sets.

# Section 5 — Defender detection

* **Wireless infrastructure logs:** AP/controller logs can record associations, authentication attempts, roaming, deauthentication events, and client behavior.
* **WIDS/WIPS sensors:** Wireless intrusion detection systems can detect rogue APs, unusual management-frame activity, unauthorized devices, and suspicious RF behavior.
* **Controller telemetry:** Enterprise controllers can correlate BSSID, client MAC, channel, RSSI, authentication state, and AP location.
* **RF monitoring:** Persistent unexpected devices or unusual channel activity can reveal unauthorized wireless infrastructure.
* **Common miss:** Defenders often monitor IP traffic but fail to monitor the wireless layer itself, allowing rogue APs and suspicious clients to remain invisible.
* **Operator footprint reduction:** Skilled operators avoid unnecessary active interaction when passive observation answers the question, because association/authentication activity creates more obvious telemetry.
* **Important distinction:** Merely observing beacon advertisements is fundamentally different from actively interacting with an AP or disrupting its clients.

# Section 6 — Lab task

**Platform:** Local Kali Linux VM with a compatible USB WiFi adapter; target a wireless network you own or have explicit permission to assess.

**Objective:** Build a correct wireless inventory identifying the SSID, BSSID, band, channel, frequency, signal strength, and supported 802.11 capabilities of at least one AP.

**Steps:**

1. Connect the USB WiFi adapter to the Kali VM.
2. Verify that Kali detects the wireless interface.
3. Confirm that the radio is not blocked.
4. Determine which frequency bands the adapter supports.
5. Discover nearby AP advertisements using a normal WiFi scan.
6. Select your own lab AP from the results.
7. Record its SSID, BSSID, frequency, channel, and signal strength.
8. Determine whether the AP advertises HT, VHT, or HE capabilities.
9. Convert the frequency into the corresponding band and verify that the channel/band relationship makes sense.
10. Save the results as a Markdown wireless inventory.

**Expected output:**

```text
SSID:        Lab-WiFi
BSSID:       XX:XX:XX:XX:XX:XX
Band:        5 GHz
Frequency:   5180 MHz
Channel:     36
Signal:      -45 dBm
Capabilities: HT / VHT / HE
```

You should be able to explain **why** 5180 MHz corresponds to 5 GHz and what the capability information tells you.

# Section 7 — Common mistakes

1. **Confusing SSID with BSSID** → SSID identifies the advertised network name while BSSID identifies a specific wireless BSS/radio → record both during enumeration.

2. **Assuming every WiFi network is 2.4 GHz** → modern deployments commonly use 5 GHz and increasingly 6 GHz → always inspect frequency/channel information.

3. **Confusing channel number with frequency** → channel 36 is not itself "5 GHz"; it maps to a particular frequency within that band → record channel and frequency together.

4. **Assuming a wider channel is automatically better** → 80/160 MHz consumes substantially more spectrum and may encounter interference → evaluate channel width together with RF conditions.

5. **Confusing adapter capability with AP capability** → `iw list` tells you what your hardware can support, not what the target AP provides → compare adapter capabilities with actual AP advertisements.

6. **Treating signal strength as a simple distance measurement** → walls, antennas, transmit power, interference, and reflections affect received power → use signal strength as an observation, not an exact distance calculator.

7. **Ignoring multiple BSSIDs using one SSID** → enterprise WLANs commonly deploy multiple APs/radios under the same SSID → map BSSIDs individually instead of treating every SSID occurrence as one device.

# Section 8 — Move-on gate

1. **Run a wireless scan against your lab AP and identify its SSID, BSSID, frequency, channel, signal level, and security configuration without looking at your notes.**

2. **Inspect your adapter capabilities and correctly identify which 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz features/channels it supports, then distinguish those capabilities from the capabilities advertised by your lab AP.**

3. **Given three AP scan results, correctly classify each as 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, or 6 GHz and explain what the channel, channel width, signal strength, and 802.11 capability information tells you about each AP.**
